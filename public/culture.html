Culture & modern history

Korea's living spirit tradition

Korean shamanism — musok — is one of the oldest threads in Korean culture, and one of the most alive today. Here's the context behind the experience.

The basics

What is Korean shamanism?

Older than Buddhism or Confucianism in Korea, musok is the country's indigenous folk spirituality — a way of keeping the human and spirit worlds in conversation.

At its center is the mudang, a shaman who serves as a bridge between people and the spirits. Mudang are predominantly women. Many are "called" to the role through an initiatory spirit-illness known as sinbyeong; others inherit it. Their work is intensely practical: to heal, to bless a household or business, to offer guidance, and to send off the dead with care.

The signature rite is the gut (also written kut) — an immersive ceremony of music, drumming, dance, vivid costume, and spirit communication that can last hours. By most estimates more than 200,000 mudang practice in South Korea today, and counts that include fortune-tellers run far higher.

A contested history

Suppressed, then reclaimed

For centuries, shamanism was pushed to the margins by whoever held power — only to endure, and recently to be embraced as cultural heritage.

1392–1897
Joseon dynasty. Confucian elites taxed and legally restricted mudang, regarding their rites as improper to a Confucian social order.
1910–1945
Japanese colonial rule. Authorities labeled shamanism a "primitive custom" and repressed it — in part because it was seen as a deeply native marker of Korean identity.
1970s
The "Movement to Overthrow Superstition." Under Park Chung-hee's Saemaul Undong (New Community Movement), police suppressed mudang; village shrines and sacred trees were destroyed and gut rites broken up.
1970s onward
Scholarly revival. A wave of folklore studies reframed musok as Korea's ancient religion, and acceptance grew among educated Koreans.
2009
Recognized as heritage. The government listed regional gut styles as intangible cultural heritage; major gut ceremonies are designated Important Intangible Cultural Properties.

Shamanism & power

An uneasy place near politics

One of the recurring themes in Korea's relationship with shamanism is paradox: officially marginalized, yet never far from public life — and, at times, from the corridors of power.

The most widely reported modern episode came in 2016, around then-President Park Geun-hye and her longtime confidante Choi Soon-sil. International and Korean media extensively covered Choi's unusual influence and her family's quasi-religious background — her late father, Choi Tae-min, was a figure some in the press described as a "shaman." Choi was later convicted of corruption and abuse of power, and Park was impeached and removed from office in 2017.

Notably, professional shamans largely distanced themselves from the affair, with many feeling it unfairly tarnished their tradition — as one put it to reporters, the scandal "gives shamans a bad name." More broadly, journalists have reported that the clientele of well-known shamans has, over the years, included senior politicians as well as business and entertainment figures.

A modern fixture

Not a relic — a habit

Far from fading away, fortune-telling and shamanic consultation are woven into contemporary Korean life. In one widely cited survey, about two in three respondents said they visit a fortune teller at least once a year; other figures put it at roughly four in ten adults having gone at least once.

Who consults them? Businesspeople pick auspicious dates to launch new ventures. Couples check their gunghap (compatibility) before marriage. Students and parents seek reassurance before exams. And reporting on Seoul's affluent Gangnam district — now a hub for upscale spiritual consulting — describes a clientele that can range from tech CEOs and K-pop idols to senior politicians. Demand for the most famous practitioners is high enough that appointments are routinely booked a month, sometimes two, in advance.

What is saju?

Many readings are based on saju, the "Four Pillars" — your birth year, month, day, and hour. Using the principles of yin-yang and the five elements, a reader interprets your character and the shape of your fortune: career, relationships, and timing. It's the gentle, conversational end of a spectrum that reaches all the way to the full intensity of a gut.
